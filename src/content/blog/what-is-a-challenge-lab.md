A challenge lab is an industry-driven research and development space where companies, universities, and students work together to solve specific technical problems. Unlike traditional academic research, challenge labs focus on real-world challenges that industry partners bring to the table, such as developing new materials, improving manufacturing processes, or prototyping new technologies. FSIC's Innovation Engine will include challenge labs focused on aerospace, advanced manufacturing, and related fields, connecting Volusia County businesses to university expertise and emerging talent.

## What is a challenge lab?

A challenge lab is a collaborative workspace designed to tackle industry-defined problems through applied research, prototyping, and testing. Companies sponsor challenges—specific technical questions or development needs—and university researchers, students, and FSIC staff work to solve them.

Challenge labs differ from traditional university research in three important ways:

1. **Industry sets the agenda**: Problems come from companies with real needs, not academic curiosity. The work has immediate commercial or operational value.
2. **Results are delivered quickly**: Projects run on timelines measured in months, not years. The goal is a working prototype, validated process, or proven concept—not a published paper.
3. **Students gain hands-on experience**: Participants work on real problems with real constraints, learning skills employers need while contributing to solutions companies will use.

Challenge labs sit at the intersection of workforce development, innovation, and economic development. They produce technical outcomes for companies, career-ready graduates for employers, and commercializable intellectual property for the region.

## What kinds of challenges do challenge labs address?

Challenge labs work on problems that are too risky, specialized, or expensive for a single company to solve alone but are common enough that multiple companies benefit from the solution. Examples include:

### Materials and manufacturing processes
- Developing lighter, stronger, or more heat-resistant materials for spacecraft or launch vehicles
- Improving additive manufacturing (3D printing) techniques for aerospace-grade metals
- Reducing defects in composite layup or welding processes
- Finding faster, cheaper ways to inspect parts without damaging them

### Automation and robotics
- Designing robotic systems for assembly, inspection, or hazardous environments
- Improving sensors and control systems for precision manufacturing
- Developing machine learning models to predict equipment failures or optimize production schedules

### Prototyping and testing
- Building and testing proof-of-concept designs for new spacecraft systems, avionics, or propulsion components
- Validating new approaches to thermal management, power distribution, or communication systems
- Creating simulations or digital twins to model system performance before hardware is built

### Sustainability and efficiency
- Reducing waste or energy use in manufacturing processes
- Developing recycling or reuse systems for aerospace materials
- Finding alternatives to scarce or environmentally harmful materials

These are not academic exercises. They are problems that companies face in their day-to-day operations or in pursuing new contracts, and solving them creates competitive advantages.

## How does a challenge lab work?

Challenge labs operate on a project-based model:

1. **A company sponsors a challenge**: The sponsor defines a problem, sets success criteria, and provides funding (or in-kind resources such as equipment, materials, or expertise).
2. **A team is assembled**: University faculty, students (undergraduate and graduate), and FSIC staff form a project team. Some challenges may include researchers from multiple institutions.
3. **The work begins**: Teams use prototyping equipment, testing facilities, and computational tools to develop solutions. Companies provide feedback, data, and access to their processes or products as needed.
4. **Results are delivered**: At the end of the project, the team presents findings, prototypes, or validated processes to the sponsor. Successful solutions may lead to patents, licensing agreements, or follow-on contracts.

Challenge labs often operate in cohorts—multiple projects running simultaneously—creating a dynamic environment where teams share equipment, ideas, and lessons learned.

## Who benefits from challenge labs?

Challenge labs serve three constituencies:

### Companies
- **Access to expertise**: University researchers and specialized equipment without the cost of hiring full-time staff or building in-house labs
- **Risk reduction**: Test new ideas at lower cost before committing to full-scale development
- **Talent pipeline**: Identify and recruit students who have already worked on relevant problems
- **Faster innovation**: Compress timelines for prototyping, testing, and validation

### Students
- **Real-world experience**: Work on problems that matter to industry, not just academic projects
- **Portfolio building**: Demonstrate skills and results to potential employers
- **Networking**: Build relationships with companies that may hire them after graduation
- **Career clarity**: Learn what kinds of problems and industries interest them before committing to a career path

### Universities
- **Industry engagement**: Strengthen connections with regional employers and align research priorities with economic needs
- **Funding**: Generate sponsored research revenue and opportunities for faculty and students
- **Reputation**: Build a track record of delivering results that companies value
- **Economic impact**: Contribute to regional innovation ecosystems and workforce development

Challenge labs turn universities into economic engines, not just credential-granting institutions.

## How will FSIC's challenge labs work?

FSIC's Innovation Engine, which includes challenge labs, is planned for Years 3–5 of the phased deployment. Challenge labs will focus on aerospace, advanced manufacturing, robotics, and related fields, drawing on expertise from universities in Central Florida and beyond.

FSIC's role will be to:

- **Recruit company sponsors**: Connect businesses with technical challenges to university researchers and students
- **Manage projects**: Coordinate teams, timelines, budgets, and deliverables
- **Provide facilities and equipment**: Offer prototyping tools, testing equipment, and computational resources that neither companies nor universities could afford alone
- **Ensure accessibility**: Make challenge labs available to small and mid-sized businesses, not just large primes with existing university partnerships
- **Capture and share lessons**: Document successful approaches, publish non-proprietary results, and help other companies adopt proven solutions

Challenge labs will operate in partnership with universities but will be structured to serve industry needs first. This ensures that the work remains practical, timely, and aligned with the region's economic priorities.

## What is the difference between a challenge lab and a university research center?

Traditional university research centers pursue long-term, curiosity-driven research that advances knowledge in a field. Faculty set the research agenda, timelines are measured in years, and success is measured by publications, citations, and grants.

Challenge labs are different:

| Aspect | University Research Center | Challenge Lab |
|--------|---------------------------|---------------|
| **Agenda** | Faculty-driven, peer-reviewed priorities | Industry-driven, sponsor-defined problems |
| **Timeline** | Multi-year research programs | Months to one year per project |
| **Success metric** | Publications, grants, citations | Working prototypes, validated processes, hired students |
| **Funding** | Federal research grants, endowments | Company sponsorships, state innovation programs |
| **Participants** | Graduate students, postdocs, faculty | Undergraduates, graduates, faculty, industry mentors |
| **Output** | Papers, theses, trained PhDs | Prototypes, processes, career-ready technicians and engineers |

Both have value, but challenge labs prioritize speed, practical outcomes, and workforce development over academic prestige.

## Why challenge labs matter for Southeast Volusia

Southeast Volusia sits next to one of the nation's most active aerospace corridors but lacks the innovation infrastructure—labs, equipment, university partnerships—that helps companies compete for advanced contracts. Challenge labs bring that infrastructure to the region, allowing local businesses to prototype, test, and innovate without relocating or outsourcing to distant research centers.

For students and career-switchers, challenge labs provide pathways into high-skill, high-wage careers by giving them hands-on experience with the technologies employers need. They bridge the gap between classroom learning and workplace readiness.

Challenge labs are a key part of FSIC's mission: ensuring that the Space Coast's growth benefits the people and businesses already here by giving them the tools to compete, innovate, and thrive.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When will FSIC's challenge labs open?

Challenge labs are part of the Innovation Engine, planned for Years 3–5 of FSIC's phased deployment. The exact timeline depends on funding, partnerships, and facility availability. FSIC will start with pilot projects and smaller-scale collaborations before building dedicated lab spaces. Early prototyping and testing programs may begin sooner, using existing partner facilities.

### Can small businesses sponsor challenge lab projects?

Yes. FSIC's challenge labs are designed to be accessible to small and mid-sized businesses, not just large corporations. Sponsorship costs and project scopes will vary depending on the challenge's complexity and duration. FSIC may also facilitate group sponsorships, where multiple companies with similar needs co-fund a project.

### Do companies own the intellectual property from challenge lab projects?

It depends on the sponsorship agreement. Typically, the sponsoring company has first rights to license or commercialize the results. If the project produces patentable inventions, ownership is negotiated upfront based on contributions, funding, and intended use. FSIC's goal is to ensure that intellectual property benefits the region, whether through company commercialization, licensing revenue to universities, or open access for other local businesses.

### Will challenge labs only focus on aerospace?

No. While aerospace and advanced manufacturing are priority areas, challenge labs can address problems in related fields such as robotics, materials science, clean energy, and automation. The focus is on industries that align with the Space Coast's economic strengths and that can support high-wage careers in Southeast Volusia.
