## What are EDA Tech Hubs?

**Tech Hubs** is the short name for the Regional Technology and Innovation Hub Program of the U.S. Economic Development Administration (EDA), part of the U.S. Department of Commerce. EDA describes its purpose as "place-based investments in regions with the assets, resources, capacity, and potential to become globally competitive in the technologies and industries of the future within approximately 10 years."

The program was enacted as part of the CHIPS and Science Act of 2022. According to EDA, the law authorized $10 billion for the program over five years, and Congress appropriated $500 million in fiscal year 2023 to launch it.

EDA states that the program "is not intended to fund basic and fundamental research." Its awards support five kinds of work: workforce development, business and entrepreneur development, technology development and maturation, infrastructure, and governance.

## Who is eligible to be a Tech Hub?

Only consortia. Under the law (15 U.S.C. § 3722a), an eligible consortium must include at least one of each of these:

- Institutions of higher education
- State, territorial, local or Tribal governments
- Industry groups or firms in relevant technology, innovation or manufacturing sectors
- Economic development organizations focused on science, technology, innovation, entrepreneurship or access to capital
- Labor organizations or workforce training organizations

EDA defines a Tech Hub as a contiguous region with a concentration of assets, capital, research and development, a labor market and infrastructure relevant to its chosen technology, "led by coordinated consortia of public, private, and academic partners."

## How do Tech Hub designation and awards work?

In two steps: designation first, then funding. The law directs the Secretary of Commerce to use "a competitive, merit-review process" and, subject to available appropriations, to designate at least 20 hubs. EDA describes what has happened so far:

- **May 2023:** EDA released its first notice and received 198 applications from consortia seeking designation.
- **October 2023:** EDA designated 31 Tech Hubs and opened a second notice, for implementation funding, to designated hubs only.
- **July 2024:** EDA awarded $504 million to 52 projects across 12 designated Tech Hubs. The other 19 each received a $500,000 Consortium Accelerator Award.
- **September 2025:** EDA released a notice of approximately $220 million, open only to those 19 hubs. Federal funds cover at most 90% of eligible costs.

EDA calls designation "a strong signal that a region's assets, resources, and capabilities position it for transformative investment." Designation alone does not come with implementation funding.

## Where does the Tech Hubs program stand now?

As of November 12, 2026, Grants.gov lists no open or forecast Tech Hubs funding notice. The most recent, dated September 19, 2025, states: "Through this NOFO, EDA will not be making any new Tech Hub designations." (A NOFO is a notice of funding opportunity.) Its final application deadline was February 18, 2026. This post does not report its results; EDA publishes program updates on its own website.

## What are NSF Engines?

**NSF Engines** is the short name for the Regional Innovation Engines program of the U.S. National Science Foundation (NSF). NSF launched it in May 2022, and the same 2022 law directs NSF to make awards for Regional Innovation Engines (42 U.S.C. § 19108). NSF says the program aims at "spurring economic growth in regions that have not fully participated in the technology boom of the past few decades."

NSF gives its Engines three core functions:

- Use-inspired research and development
- Translation of innovation results to society
- Workforce development leading to higher-wage, better quality jobs

Each Engine works within a **region of service**, which NSF defines as "the geographical area impacted by the NSF Engine's efforts and activities."

## Who can apply for an NSF Engine, and how are awards made?

NSF encourages "regional teams of innovators and ecosystem builders from industry, higher education, nonprofits, tribal nations and state and local governments" to form regional coalitions and submit proposals.

NSF makes two kinds of award:

- **NSF Engines awards:** up to $160 million over up to 10 years
- **NSF Engines development awards:** up to $1 million over up to two years, to lay the groundwork for a future Engine

Selection is competitive and runs in rounds. In NSF's second competition, applicants submitted letters of intent, 71 teams advanced in 2024, 29 semifinalists were announced in July 2025 and 15 finalists in September 2025.

## Where does the NSF Engines program stand now?

On January 29, 2024, NSF announced its first awards, to 10 teams. On July 14, 2026, it announced awards to 12 more teams across 20 states. Each team initially receives $15 million over two years, with the potential to receive up to $160 million over the next decade if it demonstrates progress on its milestones.

As of November 12, 2026, NSF's program pages list no deadline for a new competition. They direct readers to NSF's newsletter for future funding opportunities.

One first-cohort Engine is in Florida. NSF describes the region of the NSF Florida Semiconductor Engine as 23 counties "from Tampa Bay through Orlando to the 'space coast.'" NSF's page does not list the counties, so this post does not say whether Volusia County is among them.

## What does FSIC's Master Plan say about these programs?

FSIC's Master Plan names both. It states that "federal and state policy is shifting decisively toward regional innovation ecosystems," and that programs "created or expanded through the CHIPS and Science Act," the EDA Tech Hubs program, NSF Engines and state economic development initiatives, among others, "are directing billions toward regions that can demonstrate coordinated capacity across" five areas (p. 4):

- Workforce development
- Applied research
- Entrepreneurship
- Enabling infrastructure
- Cross-sector governance

The plan then states: "The Space Coast has no civilian-facing innovation hub capable of competing for these investments" (p. 4). The same argument is on the [Strategic Moment](/strategic-moment/#place-based-innovation) page.

The plan lists EDA Tech Hubs and "NSF Engines & STEM education grants" among its potential public funding sources for Years 1–5 (p. 25). It places the pursuit of "major federal funding (EDA, NSF, DoD, NASA)" in Phase 5, Full Ecosystem Integration, planned for Years 3–5 (p. 14). DoD is the Department of Defense. Each funding stream is on the [Partnership & Funding](/partnership-funding/#funding) page, and [How Will FSIC Be Funded Over Its First Five Years?](/blog/how-is-fsic-funded/) explains the full model.

## Has FSIC applied for or received either?

No. As of November 2026, FSIC is in Phase 1 (Year 1) of its plan, and it has not applied for or received a Tech Hub designation, Tech Hubs funding or an NSF Engines award. The Master Plan lists these programs as potential sources. Naming a program is not a claim of funding.

## What do these programs mean for Southeast Volusia?

Three points follow from the sources above.

**Both programs fund regions that organize, not single institutions.** A Tech Hub consortium must include higher education, government, industry, economic development and workforce members. NSF funds regional coalitions. FSIC's Master Plan describes FSIC as a "regional backbone organization that aligns employers, educators, governments, and community partners around a shared economic future" (p. 22). Its partner types include employers, K–12 school districts, state colleges and universities, local government, and economic development and workforce agencies (pp. 22–24). The plan names no partners, and none have been announced.

**The timing is long.** EDA's goal is global competitiveness "within approximately 10 years," and an NSF Engines award runs up to 10 years. Neither program had a competition open to new regions on November 12, 2026. FSIC's plan places the pursuit of major federal funding in Years 3–5, in the phase that integrates all four engines. The phases are on the [Phased Deployment](/deployment/#phase-5) page.

**The programs are models as well as funding sources.** The Master Plan's long-term vision describes a multi-university consortium "modeled on the Houston Spaceport, the NSF Engines hubs, and major research collaboratives" (p. 36). That vision is on the [Long-Term Vision](/long-term-vision/#consortium) page.
