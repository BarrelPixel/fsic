FSIC's Talent Engine will include registered apprenticeships in aerospace and advanced manufacturing trades such as avionics technician, CNC machinist, robotics technician, quality inspector, and fabricator. Apprenticeships combine classroom instruction with paid on-the-job training at employer sites, typically lasting one to four years. Participants earn wages while learning, graduate with industry-recognized credentials, and often move into full-time positions with their training employer.

## What is a registered apprenticeship?

A registered apprenticeship is a structured training program recognized by the U.S. Department of Labor or a State Apprenticeship Agency. Apprenticeships combine two types of learning:

- **On-the-job training**: Participants work at an employer's site under the supervision of experienced mentors, learning the trade by doing real work
- **Related technical instruction**: Classroom learning, typically delivered by a community college or training provider, covering the theory, math, safety, and technical knowledge the trade requires

Apprenticeships are paid from day one. Participants earn wages that increase as their skills grow, and they graduate with a nationally recognized credential that proves competency to employers across the country.

The registered apprenticeship model has been used for decades in construction trades, healthcare, and manufacturing. It is now expanding into aerospace, advanced manufacturing, information technology, and other high-growth industries.

## What aerospace and advanced manufacturing apprenticeships will FSIC offer?

FSIC's Master Plan describes apprenticeships in trades that aerospace and advanced manufacturing employers need:

### Avionics technician
Installs, tests, repairs, and maintains the electronic systems in aircraft and spacecraft, including flight controls, communication systems, navigation equipment, and instrumentation. Apprenticeships last two to four years and lead to FAA certification or industry credentials.

### CNC machinist
Operates computer numerical control (CNC) machines to produce precision parts from metal, plastic, or composite materials. Programs machines, sets up tooling, monitors production, and inspects finished parts. Apprenticeships typically last three to four years.

### Robotics and automation technician
Installs, programs, maintains, and troubleshoots industrial robots and automated manufacturing systems. Works with programmable logic controllers (PLCs), sensors, and control systems. Apprenticeships last two to three years.

### Quality inspector
Inspects parts and assemblies to ensure they meet specifications, using precision measurement tools, non-destructive testing equipment, and quality management systems. Aerospace quality inspectors often work toward AS9100 or similar certifications. Apprenticeships last one to two years.

### Fabricator and welder
Cuts, shapes, and joins metal parts to build assemblies, structures, and components for aircraft, spacecraft, and ground systems. Specializations include TIG welding, precision sheet metal work, and composite fabrication. Apprenticeships last two to four years and may lead to AWS (American Welding Society) certification.

### Manufacturing engineering technician
Supports engineers by building prototypes, conducting tests, troubleshooting production issues, and documenting processes. Works with CAD software, testing equipment, and technical documentation systems. Apprenticeships last two to three years.

The specific trades FSIC offers will depend on employer demand, partner capacity, and funding. These examples reflect the Master Plan's priorities and the Space Coast's workforce needs.

## How does an apprenticeship work?

Apprenticeships follow a structured progression:

### 1. Recruitment and placement
- FSIC and partner employers recruit candidates from high school graduates, career-switchers, veterans, and underemployed residents
- Applicants complete an interview and assessment to match skills, interests, and employer needs
- Selected apprentices are hired by the employer and begin earning wages immediately

### 2. On-the-job training
- Apprentices work full-time at the employer's site, learning the trade under the supervision of experienced journeyworkers
- Training follows a detailed work process schedule that breaks the occupation into competencies and tasks
- Apprentices progress through increasingly complex assignments as their skills develop

### 3. Related technical instruction
- Apprentices attend classes (typically evenings, weekends, or one day per week) at a community college or training center
- Instruction covers technical theory, math, blueprint reading, safety, quality standards, and tools specific to the trade
- Credit earned often applies toward an associate degree or certificate

### 4. Competency evaluations
- Employers and instructors evaluate apprentices' progress against competency standards
- Successful completion requires demonstrating proficiency in all required tasks and knowledge areas
- Wages increase as apprentices reach milestones (often annually or at 1,000-hour intervals)

### 5. Completion and credentialing
- Graduates receive a Certificate of Completion from the Department of Labor or State Apprenticeship Agency
- The credential is nationally recognized and portable across employers
- Many graduates are offered full-time positions by their training employer

## How are apprentices paid?

Apprentices are employees, not students. They earn wages from their first day on the job. Starting wages typically range from 50% to 70% of a journey-level worker's wage, increasing as competencies are achieved.

For example:
- A CNC machinist apprentice might start at fifteen to eighteen dollars per hour and reach thirty to forty dollars per hour upon completion
- An avionics technician apprentice might start at eighteen to twenty-two dollars per hour and reach forty-five to sixty dollars per hour as a journeyworker

Employers pay apprentice wages, and many also cover or subsidize the cost of related technical instruction, tools, and safety equipment. Federal and state incentive programs may reimburse employers for a portion of training costs.

## Who can participate in an apprenticeship?

Apprenticeships are open to anyone who meets the program's entry requirements, which vary by trade and employer. Common requirements include:

- **Age**: Typically 18 or older (16 or 17 for youth apprenticeships in some trades)
- **Education**: High school diploma or equivalent for most programs; some advanced trades may prefer college coursework
- **Physical ability**: Must be able to perform the essential functions of the trade, with or without reasonable accommodation
- **Aptitude**: Basic math, reading, and problem-solving skills; may be assessed through tests or interviews

Apprenticeships are designed to be accessible. Prior experience in the trade is not required—apprenticeships are learn-while-you-earn programs. Veterans, career-switchers, and adults reentering the workforce are encouraged to apply.

## How will FSIC support apprenticeships?

FSIC's Talent Engine will coordinate apprenticeships by:

- **Building employer partnerships**: Recruiting aerospace and advanced manufacturing employers to sponsor apprenticeship programs and hire apprentices
- **Matching candidates to employers**: Screening and referring qualified candidates to open apprenticeship positions
- **Delivering related instruction**: Partnering with community colleges to provide classroom training aligned to Department of Labor competency standards
- **Securing funding**: Applying for federal and state apprenticeship grants to offset employer training costs
- **Tracking outcomes**: Monitoring completion rates, job placements, and wage growth to ensure programs deliver results

FSIC's role is to make apprenticeships easier for employers to offer and easier for residents to access. Many small and mid-sized companies want to train workers but lack the capacity or expertise to run registered apprenticeship programs. FSIC provides that infrastructure.

## Why apprenticeships matter for Southeast Volusia

Apprenticeships create pathways into high-wage careers that do not require a four-year degree. For residents who cannot afford or do not want a traditional college experience, apprenticeships offer an alternative that pays from day one and leads to stable, well-paid work.

For employers, apprenticeships address workforce shortages by training workers to their specific needs. Apprentices learn the company's processes, tools, and standards, making them more productive and loyal than hires trained elsewhere.

For the region, apprenticeships retain talent. Graduates live and work locally, spend their wages in the community, and often become mentors to the next generation of apprentices. This builds a sustainable pipeline of skilled workers that keeps Southeast Volusia competitive as the Space Coast grows.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long do apprenticeships take?

Apprenticeships typically last one to four years, depending on the occupation's complexity. Simple trades (such as quality inspector) may take one to two years. Complex trades (such as CNC machinist or avionics technician) may take three to four years. Competency-based programs allow apprentices to progress faster if they demonstrate mastery ahead of schedule.

### Can apprentices work part-time?

Most registered apprenticeships require full-time employment (typically 40 hours per week) because on-the-job training is structured around workplace tasks and progression. However, some programs offer part-time or flexible schedules for participants with caregiving responsibilities, disabilities, or other constraints. FSIC will work with employers to accommodate diverse needs where possible.

### What happens if an apprentice leaves the program early?

Apprentices are employees and can leave at any time. If an apprentice leaves before completing the program, they do not receive the credential but keep the skills and wages earned. Some apprentices transfer to another employer and continue their training there. Employers and FSIC work to support apprentices through challenges (transportation, childcare, personal issues) to maximize completion rates.

### Do apprentices get benefits like health insurance?

Yes, in most cases. Apprentices are employees and typically receive the same benefits as other employees, including health insurance, paid time off, and retirement contributions. Benefits vary by employer and may be prorated for apprentices working part-time or in their first year.
