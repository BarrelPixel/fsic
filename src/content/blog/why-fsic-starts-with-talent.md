FSIC starts with the Talent Engine because the Space Coast's growth is limited by the shortage of trained workers, and training programs deliver impact faster than facilities or innovation labs. The Master Plan calls the Talent Engine "the fastest path to measurable outcomes," and its first programs—micro-credentials and dual enrollment—can launch in Year 1 with community college and school district partners already in the region.

## Why does FSIC start with workforce training?

The Space Coast is growing rapidly, with aerospace and advanced manufacturing companies expanding operations and hiring thousands of workers. But the region faces a critical constraint: there are not enough people with the technical skills these employers need.

FSIC starts with the Talent Engine because workforce training:

- **Addresses the most urgent need**: Employers cite workforce shortages as the top barrier to growth. Without trained workers, companies cannot expand, relocate, or compete for new contracts.
- **Creates immediate economic value**: A worker who completes a micro-credential or apprenticeship and gets hired within months generates wage growth, tax revenue, and reduced reliance on public assistance faster than a research lab or innovation program can.
- **Serves the broadest audience**: Talent programs reach students, career-switchers, veterans, and underemployed residents—groups that represent the largest opportunity for upward mobility in Southeast Volusia.
- **Builds the foundation for the other engines**: The Innovation Engine needs technicians for labs. The Market Engine needs skilled workers for supplier companies. The Civic Engine engages students who become the next generation of STEM talent.

The Master Plan describes this as the "phased approach": start with what the region needs most and what FSIC can deliver soonest, then build the other engines on that foundation.

## What is the current workforce shortage on the Space Coast?

The Space Coast's aerospace and advanced manufacturing sectors are expanding faster than the local workforce can keep up. Employers report persistent shortages in roles such as:

- Avionics technicians
- CNC machinists and precision manufacturing operators
- Robotics and automation technicians
- Quality inspectors and certification specialists
- Welders and fabricators
- Engineering technicians

These are not entry-level jobs, and they are not jobs that require a four-year degree. They are middle-skill, high-wage technical roles that require specialized training—exactly what the Talent Engine is designed to provide.

The shortage is not unique to Florida. Nationally, the aerospace and defense industry faces a wave of retirements and struggles to attract younger workers into technical careers. But the shortage is particularly acute on the Space Coast because of the region's rapid growth and its distance from large urban talent pools.

## Why can the Talent Engine launch faster than other parts?

Unlike the Innovation Engine or a future campus, the Talent Engine does not require FSIC to build facilities, recruit faculty, or secure accreditation. Instead, it partners with institutions that already exist in Volusia County and Central Florida:

- **Community colleges** that offer technical programs and can add aerospace-focused micro-credentials or dual-enrollment courses.
- **School districts** that have career and technical education (CTE) programs and can expand STEM pathways for high school students.
- **Workforce development agencies** that connect job-seekers to training and employers to candidates.
- **Employers** who are willing to host apprenticeships, provide equipment, and validate that training leads to real jobs.

FSIC's role is to coordinate these partners, fill gaps, and bring funding to scale up programs that already work. This is much faster than building something from scratch.

The Master Plan describes Year 1 programs as "launching with existing partners," meaning FSIC can show results—students completing credentials, adults getting hired, employers filling openings—within the first twelve months.

## What are the first Talent Engine programs?

The first programs are designed to launch quickly and serve the highest-need audiences:

- **Micro-credentials**: Short, job-focused training programs in fields such as robotics, avionics, and precision machining. These take weeks or months, not years, and lead directly to employment.
- **Dual enrollment**: Expanding high school students' access to college-level STEM courses, so they graduate with both a diploma and credits toward a technical degree or certification.
- **Apprenticeships**: Earn-while-you-learn programs that combine classroom instruction with on-the-job training, often leading to industry-recognized certifications and full-time employment.
- **Career navigation**: Advising and support to help residents understand what jobs exist, what training they need, and how to access it—especially for adults who did not follow a traditional college path.

These programs share common features: they are short, stackable (credentials build on each other), tied to employer needs, and designed for working adults and high school students. They are not meant to replace four-year degrees but to complement them by serving the majority of residents for whom a bachelor's degree is not the right path.

## How does the Talent Engine support the other engines?

The Talent Engine is not separate from FSIC's other work—it is the foundation:

- **The Innovation Engine** needs skilled technicians to operate prototyping equipment, run challenge labs, and support applied research projects. Those technicians come from Talent Engine programs.
- **The Market Engine** helps small businesses become suppliers to aerospace and advanced manufacturing companies. Those companies need trained workers. Talent Engine programs ensure a pipeline of qualified candidates.
- **The Civic Engine** engages K-12 students in STEM learning, maker programs, and public exhibitions. Those students are future Talent Engine participants—and many will go on to lead the next generation of Space Coast innovation.

In the Master Plan's later phases (Years 3–5), the Talent Engine expands to include teacher upskilling, industry-sponsored research fellowships, and programs tied to the Innovation Engine's challenge labs. But it starts with the basics: getting residents trained and hired in the jobs that exist today.

## What does success look like for the Talent Engine?

FSIC measures the Talent Engine's success by outcomes that matter to the community:

- **Completions**: The number of residents who complete micro-credentials, dual-enrollment pathways, or apprenticeships.
- **Placements**: The percentage of program completers who get hired in their field within six months.
- **Wages**: The starting wages for program completers, compared to regional averages.
- **Employer satisfaction**: Feedback from employers on whether program graduates meet their needs.
- **Equity**: The demographics of participants, ensuring that programs serve students from underrepresented groups, low-income families, and rural areas.

These are measurable, reportable outcomes that funders, partners, and the community can track. They are also outcomes that FSIC can begin reporting in Year 1, long before a campus is built or a research lab opens.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does starting with the Talent Engine mean FSIC will not build innovation programs?

No. The phased approach means FSIC starts with what the region needs most urgently and what can be delivered soonest. The Innovation Engine—challenge labs, prototyping facilities, and applied research—is part of the Master Plan's later phases (Years 3–5). Starting with Talent does not delay Innovation; it ensures that when Innovation programs launch, there is a trained workforce ready to support them.

### Will the Talent Engine only serve people who want to work in aerospace?

No. The Talent Engine focuses on skills that employers in aerospace, advanced manufacturing, and related industries need, but those skills are transferable. A CNC machinist trained for aerospace work can also work in medical device manufacturing, automotive, or industrial machinery. Robotics and automation skills apply across nearly every sector. The Talent Engine prepares residents for high-wage technical careers, not just one industry.

### How will FSIC avoid competing with community colleges?

FSIC partners with community colleges—it does not replace them. The Talent Engine co-brands programs, shares facilities and curriculum, and strengthens enrollment in partner institutions. When a student completes a micro-credential through FSIC, the credential comes from a community college, and the student earns college credits. FSIC's role is to coordinate partners, secure funding for program expansion, and connect students to employers. Community colleges remain the credential-granting institutions.

### Why not start with the Innovation Engine or a campus?

Building an innovation center or campus requires land, facilities, faculty, accreditation, and years of planning. The Talent Engine can launch within months by partnering with institutions that already exist. It also addresses the most urgent need: workforce shortages are the top barrier to growth right now, not a lack of prototyping labs. The phased approach ensures FSIC delivers value to the community as early as possible, building momentum and credibility that support later phases.
