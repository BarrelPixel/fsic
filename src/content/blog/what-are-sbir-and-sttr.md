SBIR (Small Business Innovation Research) and STTR (Small Business Technology Transfer) are federal programs that award grants and contracts to small businesses to develop innovative technologies for government and commercial use. Businesses in Volusia County working in aerospace, advanced manufacturing, or related fields can apply through SBIR.gov, and FSIC's Market Engine will help local companies understand the process and strengthen their proposals.

## What are SBIR and STTR?

SBIR and STTR are competitive federal funding programs designed to encourage small businesses to engage in research and development (R&D) with commercialization potential. Eleven federal agencies participate, including NASA, the Department of Defense, the Department of Energy, and the National Science Foundation.

Both programs award non-dilutive funding—meaning businesses receive money without giving up equity or ownership. Awards are structured in phases, starting with feasibility studies and progressing to prototype development and commercialization.

Congress established SBIR in 1982 and STTR in 1992 to support innovation-driven small businesses and ensure that federal R&D dollars reach companies capable of turning research into products and services.

## What is the difference between SBIR and STTR?

The main difference is the partnership requirement:

- **SBIR** is for small businesses working independently. The company must perform at least two-thirds of the Phase I work and at least half of the Phase II work.
- **STTR** requires a partnership with a nonprofit research institution, such as a university, federally funded R&D center, or qualified research organization. The small business must perform at least 40% of the work, and the research partner must perform at least 30%.

Both programs follow the same three-phase structure and offer similar funding amounts. STTR is designed for technologies that benefit from collaboration between businesses and research institutions, making it a good fit for university-adjacent companies or those developing advanced technologies that require academic expertise.

## Who is eligible to apply?

To qualify for SBIR or STTR, a business must meet these requirements:

- **American-owned and independently operated**: At least 51% owned by U.S. citizens or permanent residents
- **For-profit**: Organized as a corporation, partnership, or limited liability company
- **Small**: Fewer than 500 employees, including affiliates
- **Principal investigator primarily employed**: The lead researcher must work for the small business for more than half their time during the award period

For STTR, the business must also have a formal partnership agreement with a qualifying research institution before applying.

Businesses do not need to have prior federal contracts or grants to apply, and first-time applicants are common. However, proposals must demonstrate technical merit, commercial potential, and the team's capability to execute the work.

## How much funding is available?

SBIR and STTR awards are structured in three phases:

- **Phase I (Feasibility)**: Typically fifty thousand to two hundred and fifty thousand dollars over six to twelve months. This phase establishes the technical merit and feasibility of the proposed innovation.
- **Phase II (Development)**: Typically three-quarters of a million to two million dollars over two years. This phase funds prototype development, testing, and initial commercialization activities.
- **Phase III (Commercialization)**: Non-SBIR/STTR funds. The business transitions the technology to the market, often through follow-on contracts, private investment, or sales.

Funding amounts vary by agency and topic. Some agencies offer additional programs, such as Phase IB bridge funding, Phase II enhancements, or commercialization supplements. The combined potential funding can exceed three million dollars for a successful project.

## What does the application process look like?

Each participating federal agency releases solicitations—calls for proposals—on specific topics tied to the agency's mission. NASA, for example, solicits proposals on spacecraft systems, propulsion, and remote sensing. The Department of Defense solicits proposals on defense technologies, materials, and cybersecurity.

The application process typically follows these steps:

1. **Review open solicitations**: Visit SBIR.gov to find current opportunities from the eleven participating agencies. Solicitations list technical topics, eligibility requirements, and proposal deadlines.
2. **Select a topic**: Choose a topic that aligns with your technology and the agency's needs. Some agencies allow open topics; others are more prescriptive.
3. **Prepare a proposal**: Write a technical proposal describing the innovation, its commercial potential, the work plan, and the team's qualifications. Proposals typically include a project narrative, budget, and supporting documents.
4. **Submit through the agency portal**: Each agency has its own submission system and deadlines. Most agencies accept proposals once or twice per year.
5. **Evaluation**: Proposals are reviewed by technical experts. Evaluation criteria typically include innovation, technical approach, commercial potential, and qualifications of the team.
6. **Award and execution**: Successful applicants receive a contract or grant and begin work. Phase I lasts six to twelve months; companies can then apply for Phase II funding based on Phase I results.

The process is competitive. Acceptance rates vary by agency and topic but generally range from 10% to 25% for Phase I and 40% to 60% for Phase II (among Phase I recipients).

## How will FSIC help Volusia County businesses apply?

FSIC's Market Engine will provide support to local businesses pursuing SBIR and STTR funding, including:

- **Proposal workshops**: Training on how to write competitive proposals, understand agency priorities, and structure budgets.
- **Technical review**: Feedback on draft proposals from mentors with SBIR/STTR experience and subject matter experts.
- **Matchmaking**: Connecting small businesses with university researchers for STTR partnerships, particularly with institutions in Central Florida.
- **Commercialization support**: Helping Phase II awardees develop go-to-market strategies, identify customers, and attract follow-on investment.
- **Networking**: Bringing together local SBIR/STTR recipients, agency program managers, and industry partners to share lessons and opportunities.

These services are part of FSIC's broader supplier-readiness mission: helping small businesses in Volusia County access high-value contracts, certifications, and funding opportunities that are currently difficult to navigate alone.

## Why SBIR and STTR matter for the Space Coast

Southeast Volusia sits beside one of the nation's most active aerospace corridors, yet many local businesses lack the resources or know-how to compete for federal innovation funding. SBIR and STTR are particularly relevant for the region because:

- **NASA and the Department of Defense** are major SBIR/STTR funders, and their priorities align with Space Coast industries: propulsion, materials, avionics, robotics, and manufacturing.
- **Non-dilutive funding** allows businesses to develop new technologies without giving up ownership, preserving the value of successful innovations for the founders and the community.
- **Phase III commercialization** often leads to follow-on contracts with the Department of Defense, NASA, and commercial aerospace customers—markets that are growing rapidly in Florida.

FSIC's role is to demystify the process, connect businesses to the resources they need, and build a track record of successful applicants in Southeast Volusia. Over time, this increases the region's capacity to attract and retain innovation-driven companies.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a business apply for both SBIR and STTR?

Yes. A business can submit proposals to both programs, even on the same topic, as long as the proposed work is distinct. Some agencies allow the same technology to be proposed under both programs in different solicitations, but applicants should check the agency's rules.

### How long does the application process take?

From proposal submission to award notification typically takes four to six months, depending on the agency. The full cycle—from identifying a solicitation to starting Phase I work—can take six to nine months. Businesses should plan ahead and watch for upcoming solicitation deadlines on SBIR.gov.

### What happens if my proposal is selected?

If your Phase I proposal is selected, you will negotiate a contract or grant with the agency, then begin work. At the end of Phase I (usually six to twelve months), you submit a final report. If the results are promising, you can apply for Phase II funding to continue development. Phase II awards are larger and longer (typically two years), and successful Phase II projects can lead to Phase III commercialization, follow-on contracts, or private investment.

### Do I need a patent to apply?

No. You do not need a patent to apply for SBIR or STTR funding. However, you should have a clear understanding of the intellectual property landscape related to your technology and a plan for protecting your innovations if the project is successful. Proposals are evaluated on technical merit, feasibility, and commercial potential—not on whether a patent already exists.
