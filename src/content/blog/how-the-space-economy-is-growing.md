The global space economy is projected to grow from roughly six hundred thirty billion dollars in 2023 to one point eight trillion dollars by 2035, driven by lower launch costs, mass-produced satellites, AI-enabled applications, and rising demand for space-based data. This growth is creating thousands of high-wage jobs in aerospace and advanced manufacturing on Florida's Space Coast. Southeast Volusia sits beside this boom but currently captures little of its value. FSIC's mission is to ensure the region participates fully by training workers, supporting businesses, and building innovation infrastructure.

## What is driving the growth of the space economy?

Four forces are transforming the space industry from a government-dominated sector into a fast-growing commercial market:

### 1. Lower launch costs
A decade ago, launching a satellite cost tens of thousands of dollars per kilogram. Today, reusable rockets from SpaceX and other providers have reduced costs to a few thousand dollars per kilogram—and prices continue to fall.

Lower costs make space accessible to industries that could not afford it before: agriculture, logistics, insurance, telecommunications, and climate monitoring. This expands the customer base and drives demand for satellites, ground systems, and data services.

### 2. Mass production of satellites
Satellites used to be custom-built, multi-year projects costing hundreds of millions of dollars each. Today, companies build small satellites (called smallsats or CubeSats) on assembly lines, launching hundreds or thousands at a time to create constellations that provide global coverage.

Mass production requires manufacturing expertise, automation, and supply chains—capabilities that Southeast Volusia's existing workforce and businesses can provide if they are trained and certified.

### 3. AI and data analytics
Satellites generate vast amounts of data: imagery, weather patterns, crop health, ship movements, infrastructure changes. Artificial intelligence and machine learning turn this data into actionable insights for businesses, governments, and researchers.

The value is no longer just in launching satellites—it is in analyzing and selling the insights those satellites produce. This creates jobs in software, data science, and IT, not just hardware manufacturing.

### 4. New applications across industries
Space is no longer just about rockets and astronauts. Industries across the economy now rely on space-based services:

- **Agriculture**: Satellite imagery helps farmers optimize irrigation, detect disease, and predict yields
- **Logistics**: GPS and tracking systems monitor shipments and optimize routes
- **Insurance**: Satellite data assesses disaster risk, tracks claims, and validates damages
- **Climate science**: Earth observation satellites monitor deforestation, sea-level rise, and carbon emissions
- **National security**: Defense and intelligence agencies use satellite reconnaissance, communication, and navigation

As more industries discover what space-based data can do, demand grows—and the jobs required to support that demand grow with it.

## What does this mean for Florida's Space Coast?

Florida's Space Coast—the region stretching from Brevard County north to Volusia County—is experiencing unprecedented growth:

- **More launches**: Cape Canaveral and Kennedy Space Center host dozens of launches annually, more than any other location in the world
- **Expanding facilities**: SpaceX, Blue Origin, United Launch Alliance, and smaller companies are building manufacturing plants, test facilities, and launch pads
- **Hiring surges**: Aerospace employers are adding thousands of workers in engineering, manufacturing, quality assurance, and technical support

But growth creates challenges:

- **Workforce shortages**: Employers struggle to find trained workers in avionics, machining, welding, robotics, and other technical roles
- **Housing and infrastructure strain**: Rapid population growth strains roads, schools, and services
- **Economic inequality**: High-wage aerospace jobs go to workers brought in from elsewhere, while many local residents remain underemployed or stuck in low-wage service work

FSIC exists to address these challenges by ensuring that Southeast Volusia's residents, students, and businesses can participate in—and benefit from—the Space Coast's boom.

## How much of this growth is Southeast Volusia capturing?

Very little. Most aerospace jobs and contracts flow to Brevard County or out of state. Southeast Volusia's residents commute north for work, businesses lack the certifications to compete for contracts, and students leave the region for training and careers elsewhere.

The Master Plan calls this "the proximity problem": Southeast Volusia sits next door to one of the fastest-growing economic sectors in the country but does not participate proportionally in its value.

FSIC's role is to change that by:

- **Training local residents** for aerospace and advanced manufacturing careers, so they can work for Space Coast employers without leaving the region
- **Helping local businesses** achieve supplier certifications and win contracts, keeping more economic activity in Southeast Volusia
- **Building innovation infrastructure** (challenge labs, prototyping facilities) so companies can innovate locally rather than outsourcing R&D to distant research centers
- **Engaging students and families** in STEM, making aerospace careers visible and accessible to the next generation

## What kinds of jobs is the space economy creating?

Space industry jobs fall into three broad categories:

### 1. Hardware manufacturing
- **Roles**: CNC machinists, welders, fabricators, composites technicians, quality inspectors, avionics technicians
- **Skills**: Precision manufacturing, blueprint reading, CAD/CAM, materials expertise, quality standards (AS9100)
- **Education**: High school diploma plus technical training or apprenticeship; some roles require an associate degree
- **Wages**: Typically thirty to sixty dollars per hour for journey-level workers

### 2. Engineering and design
- **Roles**: Mechanical engineers, electrical engineers, systems engineers, software developers, test engineers
- **Skills**: Design, analysis, simulation, testing, problem-solving, project management
- **Education**: Bachelor's degree or higher in engineering, computer science, or related fields
- **Wages**: Typically seventy thousand to one hundred twenty thousand dollars annually for early-career engineers; higher for experienced or specialized roles

### 3. Data and IT services
- **Roles**: Data analysts, software developers, cybersecurity specialists, IT infrastructure support
- **Skills**: Programming, data science, machine learning, cloud computing, cybersecurity (CMMC)
- **Education**: Associate or bachelor's degree in IT, computer science, data science, or related fields; certifications valued
- **Wages**: Typically sixty thousand to one hundred thousand dollars annually

The largest employment growth is in hardware manufacturing and technical support roles—jobs that require training but not necessarily a four-year degree. This is where FSIC's Talent Engine focuses.

## Is the space economy's growth sustainable?

Growth projections depend on assumptions about technology, demand, and investment. Key risks include:

- **Economic downturns**: A recession could reduce private investment in space startups and satellite constellations
- **Regulatory barriers**: Export controls, spectrum allocation, and orbital debris regulations could slow deployment
- **Launch failures or accidents**: High-profile failures could erode investor confidence or trigger regulatory restrictions
- **Market saturation**: Some analysts question whether demand for satellite data will grow fast enough to justify the number of satellites being launched

However, industry leaders and analysts believe the fundamentals are strong:

- Falling costs make space economically viable for a growing range of applications
- Government and commercial demand for satellite data continues to rise
- New industries (space tourism, asteroid mining, lunar exploration) are emerging, creating additional growth paths

The consensus is that the space economy will continue to grow, though the pace and size may vary from current projections.

## What happens if Southeast Volusia does not act?

If Southeast Volusia does not build workforce capacity, supplier infrastructure, and innovation systems, the Space Coast's growth will continue to bypass the region. Residents will remain locked out of high-wage careers, businesses will miss contracts, and the economic benefits of proximity will flow elsewhere.

FSIC is the region's response: a coordinated effort to ensure that Southeast Volusia participates in the space economy, not just watches it from the sidelines.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is the space economy only about rockets and satellites?

No. The space economy includes everything required to build, launch, operate, and use space-based systems: manufacturing, software, data services, logistics, finance, insurance, and legal services. Many "space economy" jobs are in earthbound industries that support or use space-based capabilities.

### Will the space economy create jobs for people without college degrees?

Yes. Many technical roles in manufacturing, quality assurance, and technical support require training but not a four-year degree. Apprenticeships, micro-credentials, and associate degrees can lead to high-wage careers. FSIC's Talent Engine focuses on these pathways because they serve the broadest population and address the most urgent workforce shortages.

### How does Southeast Volusia compete with Brevard County for space economy jobs?

Southeast Volusia does not compete directly—it partners. Brevard County hosts major employers and launch facilities, but those employers need suppliers, trained workers, and innovation partners. Southeast Volusia can provide those, capturing value as part of the Space Coast ecosystem. FSIC's role is to position the region as a capable, reliable partner, not a competitor.

### What if the space economy's growth slows?

FSIC's strategy is not dependent on one industry. The skills trained (precision manufacturing, robotics, automation, data analytics) apply across aerospace, advanced manufacturing, defense, medical devices, and other high-tech sectors. If aerospace growth slows, FSIC's programs remain relevant for other industries. Diversification protects against over-reliance on any single sector.
