## What role does the Master Plan give school districts?

FSIC's Master Plan names **K–12 school districts** as one of seven types of partner. In its words, "FSIC partners with school districts to expand STEM access, strengthen career pathways, and support teacher development." STEM stands for science, technology, engineering and mathematics.

The plan gives school districts three partnership roles:

- Co-design dual-enrollment and micro-credential pathways
- Provide student access to challenge labs and innovation programs
- Support teacher upskilling and professional development

Each partner type's roles are on the [Partnership & Funding](/partnership-funding/#k12) page.

## What would districts co-design?

**Dual enrollment** lets students earn college credit while they are still in school. The Master Plan describes FSIC's dual-enrollment STEM pathways as partnerships with state colleges and universities in engineering technology, advanced manufacturing, computer science and cybersecurity, and aerospace technology, in which "students earn college credit while gaining hands-on experience."

A **micro-credential** is a short, focused credential that shows someone has a specific skill. The plan describes FSIC's micro-credential academies as "short, stackable credentials aligned to employer needs," in fields such as robotics and automation, avionics fundamentals and precision machining. [What Is a Micro-Credential?](/blog/what-is-a-micro-credential/) explains them in more detail.

## What are challenge labs, and how would students take part?

A **challenge lab** is a team project on a real problem. The Master Plan describes teams working "on real aerospace and advanced manufacturing problems provided by employers, universities, or federal partners," with outcomes that include prototypes, research findings and new technologies. Challenge labs belong to FSIC's Innovation Engine, which the plan describes as enabling collaboration among "students, educators, researchers, startups, and industry partners."

The plan asks districts to provide student access to these labs. It does not say which grades or how students would be selected.

## What do districts gain?

The Master Plan describes what school districts gain as "a coordinated STEM and workforce development partner that expands opportunities for students and teachers while reducing the burden on individual schools to build specialized programs." The key benefits it lists for districts are:

- Micro-credentials and dual-enrollment pathways
- Hands-on learning and industry exposure
- Teacher upskilling and professional development
- Access to challenge labs and innovation programs
- The Regional Talent Marketplace for students

The plan also lists improved student outcomes, stronger career readiness, expanded access to high-value opportunities and enhanced district competitiveness. Its message to educators is: "We expand opportunities for your students and teachers."

## What does FSIC plan for teachers and younger students?

Teacher upskilling is one of the Talent Engine's programs: training for educators in emerging technologies, industry-aligned curriculum, hands-on lab instruction and STEM teaching methods. In the plan's words, "this expands regional teaching capacity and improves program quality."

The Talent Engine also includes "K–12 early exposure and STEM exploration," though the plan does not describe that program or give it a phase. Through FSIC's Civic Engine, the plan describes community STEM nights as "hands-on events for families and youth," and public exhibitions that showcase student projects. More on the Talent Engine is on the [FSIC Model](/model/#talent) page.

## When would these programs begin?

Phase 1, Foundation & Talent Engine Launch, is planned for Year 1. Its activities include establishing dual-enrollment STEM pathways, launching micro-credential academies, beginning teacher upskilling programs and deploying the Regional Talent Marketplace. One of its expected outcomes is "strong early partnerships with K–12, colleges, and employers." The Year 1 startup budget includes program delivery for "micro-credentials, dual enrollment, teacher training," and the Year 1 milestones include hosting initial community STEM events.

Challenge labs come with Phase 2, Innovation Engine Activation (Years 1–2). For Year 2, the plan lists increasing teacher upskilling programs, with "expanded student and teacher participation" as an expected outcome.

As of November 2026, FSIC is in Phase 1, and program details and dates have not been announced. The phases are on the [Phased Deployment](/deployment/#phase-1) page.

## How will FSIC measure the results?

The Master Plan's Talent Engine metrics include dual-enrollment participation and credit attainment, the number of students enrolled in micro-credentials, completion and certification rates, and teacher upskilling participation. They are reported through quarterly program dashboards, an annual workforce impact report and employer advisory council reviews. The plan's first evaluation principle is to "measure what matters most to employers, educators, funders, and the community."

The plan also lists an Education and Workforce Advisory Council among FSIC's advisory councils. It does not say who will sit on it. The councils are described on the [Governance & Transparency](/governance/#structure) page.

## How can a district start a conversation now?

By email, at info@fsic.space. The [Contact](/contact/) page opens an email with the subject filled in for a partnership inquiry. The ways the public can take part are in the FAQ answer [How can the public get involved?](/faq/#get-involved), and the programs planned first are in [What kinds of programs will FSIC offer first?](/faq/#first-programs)

## What has not been decided yet?

The Master Plan does not name partner districts, set terms or costs for them, or say which grades, schools or courses the programs will serve. None of these have been announced.
