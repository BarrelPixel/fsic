## What does return on investment mean?

**Return on investment (ROI)** compares what something returns with what it costs. It is the net benefit (the benefit minus the cost) divided by the cost, usually shown as a percentage. A program that costs $1 and returns $1.50 in benefits has an ROI of 50 percent.

The arithmetic is simple. The hard part is deciding which costs and which benefits to count, and over what period.

## Why is ROI harder to state for a workforce program?

A workforce program has more than one investor and more than one beneficiary. Funders pay for it, employers can contribute time and equipment, and participants give their own time. The benefits can go to the participant, to the employer that hires them, and to the community.

So an ROI figure for a workforce program depends on whose return is counted. FSIC's Master Plan names three groups: it expects FSIC to generate "a high return on investment for funders, employers, and the community."

## What does FSIC's Master Plan say about return on investment?

The plan says the return "is both immediate and long-term, with measurable impacts across job creation, wage growth, business development, and community vitality." Its return-on-investment table links outcomes in five areas to economic implications:

- **Workforce and talent:** more job-ready graduates, higher placement rates in high-value careers and less hiring friction for employers, leading to higher regional wages, stronger employer retention and less reliance on imported labor.
- **Employers and industry:** a reliable talent pipeline and lower recruitment and training costs, leading to more employer investment, more job creation and an expanded tax base.
- **Small businesses and suppliers:** more SBIR/STTR submissions and awards and more local companies entering aerospace and defense supply chains, leading to more contract wins, higher business revenue and greater economic diversification.
- **Innovation and research:** more prototypes and research projects and stronger university partnerships, leading to more federal research funding and stronger intellectual property generation.
- **Community and civic life:** more participation in STEM events and wider access to hands-on learning, leading to community vitality, a better quality of life and the attraction of families and talent.

SBIR and STTR are the federal Small Business Innovation Research and Small Business Technology Transfer programs. STEM stands for science, technology, engineering and mathematics. The full table is on the [Economic Impact](/economic-impact/#roi) page.

## Does the plan give an ROI figure?

No. The Master Plan describes FSIC's return as outcomes and their effects on the regional economy. It does not state an ROI ratio, and its five-year budget describes expenses and revenue sources without dollar amounts.

The plan's 25-year figures are not an ROI either. For the Extended FSIC (FSIC 2.0) scenario, it projects **$10.2–$12.4 billion** in economic output, **$1.35–$1.55 billion** in new wages and **$690–$910 million** in fiscal revenue for local and state governments. These are projections for the regional economy, not returns measured against a stated cost. They are estimates based upon assumptions that may or may not be accurate over time. The [FAQ](/faq/#economic-impact) summarizes them, and [What Is a Job-Year?](/blog/what-is-a-job-year/) explains how to read one of them.

## How does the plan propose to measure the return?

Through metrics and public reports. "Demonstrate clear return on investment" is one of the guiding principles of the plan's evaluation framework, along with using data for continuous improvement and aligning metrics "with state and federal funding requirements."

The plan sets metrics for each engine. For the Talent Engine, which trains workers, they are:

- students enrolled in micro-credentials
- completion and certification rates
- dual-enrollment participation and credit attainment
- teacher upskilling participation
- internship, apprenticeship and job placement rates
- employer satisfaction and alignment

Talent Engine results are planned to be reported in quarterly program dashboards and an annual workforce impact report. The plan's public reporting tools include an Annual Impact Report and dashboards on FSIC's website. [How Will FSIC Measure Its Economic Impact?](/blog/how-fsic-measures-economic-impact/) lists the metrics and reporting cadence for all four engines.

As of November 2026, FSIC is in Phase 1 of its five-year plan, and these are plans. The Master Plan does not give dates for the first reports.

## What do federal workforce programs report?

Federally funded workforce programs under the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA) are measured against primary indicators of performance set out in federal regulation (20 CFR 677.155). For adult and dislocated worker programs, they include:

- the share of participants employed in the second quarter after leaving the program, and in the fourth quarter
- the median earnings of participants employed in the second quarter after leaving
- the share who earn a recognized postsecondary credential, or a secondary school diploma or its equivalent, during the program or within a year after
- the share making measurable skill gains toward a credential or employment during a program year

These indicators measure results, not ROI. The Master Plan lists state workforce development grants among FSIC's potential funding sources, but it does not say which federal or state reporting requirements FSIC's programs will follow.
